Kumano Kodō: The Path of Rebirth & Primeval Mists

熊野古道 ｜ 蟻の熊野詣と甦りの聖地

"Over a thousand years of moss-covered cobblestone, steep cedar roots, and sacred watercourses—where emperors walked barefoot beside mountain ascetics, shedding their worldly sins in the primeval forest."

Daimon-zaka: The 600-meter ancient stone stairway flanked by 800-year-old Meoto-sugi cedars, Wakayama Prefecture.

The Emperor’s Mud: The Legend of Ari-no-Kumano-Mōde

In the golden twilight of the Heian period (794–1185 AD), Kyoto’s imperial aristocracy became obsessed with a concept that terrified their gilded courts: *Mappō*, the prophesied Buddhist apocalypse in which the dharma would decay and souls would wander endlessly without salvation. To escape this fate, retired emperors, court ladies, and cloistered sovereigns undertook what was considered the most grueling physical penance in the realm—the *Kumano Gokō* (Imperial Pilgrimages to Kumano).

From Emperor Uda in 907 AD to Emperor Go-Shirakawa—who made the tortuous 600-kilometer round trip across the Kii mountain spine thirty-four separate times—royalty discarded their lacquered carriages, shedding aristocratic rank to trek barefoot through mud, swollen rivers, and leech-infested ravines. So ceaseless was the line of pilgrims marching single-file through the primeval rainforest that chroniclers marvelled at the sight, coining the immortal phrase **Ari-no-Kumano-mōde**—"The pilgrimage of marching ants."

Philosophical Concept: Kishikaisei (起死回生)

The Shinto-Buddhist Syncretism of "Rebirth"

Unlike Western pilgrimages that commemorate a single saint’s historical footsteps, Kumano Kodō was designed as an allegorical death and reincarnation. The rugged mountains represented the Buddhist Pure Land of the afterlife (Fudaraku). When an ascetic climbed the razor-edge ridges of the Nakahechi trail and plunged into the thermal sulfur of Yunomine, they were considered to have ritually died; emerging at Kumano Hongū Taisha, they were reborn into the world purified.

The Legend of Oguri Hangan & The Seven-Shift Springs

Nowhere is this regenerative folklore more tangible than at Yunomine Onsen. Local legend recalls the fourteenth-century warrior Oguri Hangan, who was poisoned by his prospective father-in-law, his body rotting and reduced to a sightless, limbless husk known as a *gaki* (hungry ghost). His devoted lover Terute-hime placed his withered body into a wooden cart, hauling him hundreds of miles along the rocky Kumano trail.

Upon reaching Yunomine, the local Yamabushi mountain monks submerged Oguri in the bubbling sulfur pools of **Tsuboyu**. After forty-nine days of thermal immersion in water that shifted color seven times a day, the toxins purged from his marrow, his sight returned, and his skin was restored to health. To this day, pilgrims submerge their aching muscles in the very same stone cabin, breathing in vapors that have sustained travelers for 1,800 unbroken years.

Mount Fuji & Motosu: The Sacred Mirror & Highland Fire

富士五湖 ｜ 本栖湖の逆さ富士と森の焚き火

"From the fevered white-robed ascetics of the Edo Fujikō to modern open-air lakeside campfires—tracing how Japan’s sacred volcano transformed from an object of trembling awe into the world's most contemplative wilderness refuge."

Lake Motosu at 05:40 AM: The crisp glass mirror capturing the iconic Sakasafuji, immortalized on Japan's 1,000-yen banknote.

From Terror to Worship: The Birth of the Fujikō Cult

For centuries, Mount Fuji was not viewed as a serene landscape postcard, but as a violent, incandescent deity capable of drowning entire provinces in molten rock and volcanic ash. Following the cataclysmic Jōgan eruption of 864 AD—which poured lava across primeval valleys and gave birth to the labyrinthine Aokigahara Forest—the Imperial Court recognized Fuji as the embodiment of Asama no Okami (Konohanasakuya-hime), building sacred shrines at its foot to pacify the earth’s fury.

By the Edo period (1603–1867), this dread evolved into a widespread cultural phenomenon known as **Fujikō (富士講)**. Led by ascetic masters like Kakugyō, who meditated standing for years inside freezing lava caverns, ordinary townspeople formed mutual-aid pilgrimage guilds. Dressed in unbleached white linen robes (*Shiro-shōzoku*), chanting *"Rokkon Shōjō"* (Purification of the Six Senses), tens of thousands traveled along the Tokaido road on foot, lodging in thatched pilgrim inns (*Oshi*) in Fujiyoshida before scaling the summit to witness *Goraikō*—the rebirth of the sun.

Historical Evolution: The Shift in Travel Philosophy

How Japanese Wanderers Redefined Fuji Travel

In feudal times, approaching Fuji was a matter of spiritual survival and purification. In modern Japan, however, the pilgrimage has pivoted toward slow deceleration. Instead of hurried peak-bagging, today’s discerning travelers practice "Lakeside Campfire Solitude" and "Vanlife Stillness." By perching on the secluded western shore of Lake Motosu—free from neon signage and commercial tour buses—they kindle fragrant larch logs, boil mountain coffee in cast-iron kettles, and watch the mountain's changing silhouette from dawn to starlight.

What Global Travelers Can Experience Today

For international explorers seeking more than fleeting snapshot tourism, the Fuji Five Lakes region offers an incomparable sensory convergence: navigating subterranean ice tunnels formed twelve centuries ago, tasting Michelin-starred game roasted over binchōtan charcoal, soaking in outdoor cedar baths infused with volcanic minerals, and cruising silent emerald waters at first light.
